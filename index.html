Bluestone Creamery & Coffee

Period: June 2026  |  Incorporates receipt-level revenue analysis and margin evolution review  |  Prepared July 2026

Executive Verdict

June was the strongest month in the company's recorded history, and the improvement behind it is real — but it is a different improvement than the headline suggests. Revenue of $114,240 ran slightly below June last year; adjusted profit rose about three and a half times. The engine is cost, not demand: prime cost — ingredients plus labor together — has fallen almost sixteen points of revenue against the first half of last year, verified independently against actual vendor payments and payroll cash rather than the accounting entries. Cash finished at $88,470, the highest in eighteen months and nearly double the March forecast.

Receipt-level analysis completed this month adds essential context on revenue. The year-to-date increase of 4.6% is a calendar effect: the store added Tuesday trading this year, worth $54,600, while revenue on comparable days fell 5.1%. Underneath, the mix is shifting — coffee is up 31% and growing every single month, while scoop transactions are down 16% at unchanged prices and the evening trade is eroding. The business is becoming a morning coffee-and-bakery café that also sells ice cream. That is not a problem — coffee is higher-margin and far less seasonal — but it should become a deliberate strategy, and it directly shapes what the second location is designed to be.

Two things temper the month. Reported profit of $41,180 becomes roughly $31,500 after known bookkeeping corrections, and about $23,600 of merchandise stock sits on the balance sheet against sales that have largely stopped. Neither changes the direction; both need attention before the autumn.

Revenue — What Is Actually Growing

Added Tuesday trading+$54,600
Decline on days open in both years−$28,900
Reported revenue growth+$25,700 · +4.6%

Opening Tuesdays was a sound decision — the revenue lands on a largely fixed cost base — but it is a lever that can only be pulled once. Any plan built on comparable-day performance starts from the honest number.

Coffee is the growth engine, up 31% and above prior year in every month of 2026 — and it holds through the winter far better than ice cream, which directly softens the November cash risk. Bakery, invisible in the accounting system until this analysis, is a quarter of the business and growing; the plan to move baking off site touches 24% of revenue, so the bought-in product has to be good. Ice cream is the decline: scoop transactions fell 16% at flat prices — fewer customers choosing ice cream, concentrated in evenings (−8.1%) and weekends (Saturday −6.5%, Sunday −9.6%).

Why scoop traffic is down is the most important open question in the business — competition, menu fatigue, pricing perception, or the same customers shifting spend to coffee and pastry. The data locates the decline precisely; it cannot explain it. It deserves an answer before the second location, which carries the ice cream brand, is committed.

Margin — The Story Of The Year

Prime cost as % of revenue — trailing twelve months

60% CAUTION 55% STRONG
JULAUGSEPOCTNOVDECJANFEBMARAPRMAYJUN

Prime cost — ingredients plus labor — is the standard measure of a food operation's engine room. Above 60% of revenue a concept struggles regardless of what else is done well; strong operators hold 55% or below. Bluestone spent most of 2025 above the caution line; every month of 2026 has been below it, and it has fallen every month since January. This near-sixteen-point improvement on falling comparable-day demand is why the same store that lost money in the first half of 2025 is solidly profitable now, and it is the foundation the second-location economics rest on.

Ingredient cost runs roughly ten points below prior year in every month, verified against actual vendor payments: the business paid $22,140 less for food and supplies this quarter than the same quarter last year, on slightly higher sales, through a deliberate shift from broadline distribution to direct and specialty suppliers. The improvement is structural, and conservatively stated — the books record more cost than the payments support.

The payroll question, answered. The labor line carries three posting distortions — August 2025 inflated by last year's accrual errors, October 2025 understated by $26,400 of wages parked below the operating line, and March–April 2026 tangled by accrual reversals. After adjusting all three, the improvement is genuine and independently confirmed: the point-of-sale system shows the same 27 employees processed 9% more orders while payroll cash fell about 15% — shorter shifts and better scheduling, not headcount cuts. The true 2026 labor run-rate is roughly 26–28% of revenue; planning should use that figure, not the flattered 20–23% the raw books show for May and June.

Beyond The Ledger

Your accounting system knows the dollars. It doesn't know what happened.

A P&L can tell you revenue rose 4.6%. It can't tell you an added trading day brought in more than the whole increase while sales on the days open in both years fell 5.1%. The work starts from your books. Where it helps answer a question, I add data from systems you already use and reconcile it to the ledger.

Sales detail

Line-item revenue

Receipt, order or invoice lines from your point-of-sale system, online store or invoicing, reconciled to the ledger: product or job mix, transaction counts, average ticket, timing. Where a revenue number becomes a reason.

Vendor payments

Cost, verified

What actually left the bank for materials and supplies, checked against what the books recorded — so a sourcing win can be proven rather than asserted.

Payroll & scheduling

Hours against output

Payroll cash and hours against orders produced. The difference between better scheduling and quietly losing people shows up here.

Jobs, bookings, pipeline

Forward demand

Backlog, win rates, unbilled work and job-level yield from the systems your crews already use — the leading indicators a P&L structurally cannot see. For an HVAC or trades company: job margin, technician hours against revenue, service-agreement revenue and the shoulder-season cash dip.

ERP & inventory

Cost, by product line

Standard against actual cost, purchase-price and usage variances, inventory aging and work in progress from your ERP, reconciled to the ledger. Where a margin change becomes a product line, a supplier or a plant.

Nearly all of this data already sits in systems you pay for every month. It's added read-only or as a monthly export, and only where it answers a question.

Questions

The things owners ask first.

Is this the same as hiring a fractional or outsourced CFO?

No. A fractional or outsourced CFO joins your leadership team and runs finance: cash, banks, lenders, the finance staff. I don't, and I never move money or sign for the company. I run the analysis a good CFO would run: your model rebuilt each month, risks named and tracked, recommendations priced in dollars, and a monthly meeting where I tell you what I'd do. You make the calls. If you need someone to run finance, hire a CFO; this also works alongside one.

Do you replace my bookkeeper, controller or CPA?

No. Your CPA keeps tax and filings. Your bookkeeper or controller keeps the books and the monthly reporting; I take their close and model what comes next. I don't redo it: where a figure doesn't tie to bank, payroll or vendor payments, I list the item, its profit effect and the source, and they decide the entry. If you'd rather hand me the books, I can keep them.

What if my books are a mess?

Common. Within the first week I tell you what's usable. If the books can't support the analysis yet, I can keep them myself or work alongside your bookkeeper to catch them up, quoted separately, and I tell you when the 15 days start. The specimen brief on this page found bookkeeping corrections that cut year-to-date profit by a net $41,150, and kept them apart from real performance.

What do I get from the 30-minute call?

A plain read on what I'd look at first in your numbers, what read-only access it takes, and whether your books can support the analysis now or need catching up first. If it's a fit, I quote a fixed monthly fee after the call, typically $1,000 to $3,000 a month. What does the AI actually do?

It runs the analysis. Each month the system rebuilds your financial model, reconciles the figures, and names every risk and opportunity it identifies — including the ones that contradict the headline. It doesn't log into your accounts, doesn't move anything, and doesn't decide. I review every finding, correct what's wrong, and sign the brief. You set the strategy.

Who sees our books, and what happens to the data?

Me and the software, plus the AI provider that drafts findings from calculated figures. Analysis access is read-only and you can remove it at any time. Your data isn't used to train AI models: the model runs under commercial terms that exclude it.

What does it cost, and how long is the commitment?

A fixed monthly fee, typically $1,000 to $3,000 a month depending on the size of the business and what you want answered. I quote it after the call once I've seen the state of your books, and it's never a share of anything. Any catch-up bookkeeping is quoted separately. There's no long-term contract: you can stop after the first deep analysis and keep it; after that it's month to month, with no lock-in.

What size company is this for?

Owner-run companies from roughly $1M to $100M in revenue, in any sector. Below that the analysis usually outruns the decisions it would inform.
